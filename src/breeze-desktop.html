What happens after the click

Alice Chen · March 2026

A browser is the doorway people walk through after clicking a link. For thirty years the doorway has behaved the same way regardless of what waits on the other side: open a tab, render the document, stop.

The cost of that indifference shows up as tab sprawl, downloads that vanish into folders, and bookmark lists nobody revisits. The work of keeping context falls entirely to the person doing the browsing — a burden documented at length but rarely designed against.

Reading this in a browser that understood it was an article would already be a different experience.

What would it mean to treat a product page, a research paper, a video, and a pull request as genuinely different kinds of arrival? Not with a chat box bolted to the side, but with the surface itself knowing what it is holding. A talk from last spring made the case better than most product decks.

The answer is not more features. It is fewer decisions asked of the reader. A download that remembers where it came from. A session that resumes without ceremony. A queue that turns saving into finishing. Even a shopping page is a kind of document, and it deserves to be treated as one.

Thirty years of browsers have optimised for the moment of arrival. The interesting work now sits in everything that comes after it.
